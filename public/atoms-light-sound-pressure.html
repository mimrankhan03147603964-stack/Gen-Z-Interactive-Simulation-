⚛️ Atoms & Molecules building blocks of matter

Explore the inner atomic structure with active electron orbits, build 3D rotating molecules, or observe particle phase transitions.

📖 Definition

Atoms are the fundamental units of chemical elements, consisting of a central nucleus (protons and neutrons) surrounded by orbiting electrons. Molecules consist of two or more atoms bound together by covalent or ionic bonds.

Teacher Note: Water is H₂O (2 hydrogen + 1 oxygen). Carbon dioxide is CO₂ (1 carbon + 2 oxygen). Matter transitions between solid, liquid, and gas as temperature changes.

💡 Explanation

Electrons occupy discrete quantum energy shells (K: up to 2e⁻, L: up to 8e⁻, M: up to 8e⁻). Protons define the atomic number Z, while neutrons provide nuclear binding stability.

  • Protons (p⁺): Positively charged nuclear particles.
  • Neutrons (n⁰): Neutral nuclear particles that stabilize the nucleus.
  • Electrons (e⁻): Negatively charged leptons orbiting in quantized shells.

🧩 Types of Matter

Elements

Pure substances composed of identical atoms (e.g., Gold, Carbon, Neon).

Molecules & Compounds

Chemically bonded combinations (e.g., H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, NaCl).

States of Matter

Solids vibrate in fixed lattices, liquids slide past each other, gases fly freely.

💡 Light reflection, refraction, lenses & spectrum

Interactive optical bench with laser source, plane mirror, glass block, convex focusing lens, and dispersion prism.

📖 Definition

Light is transverse electromagnetic radiation. In optical media, light reflects at equal angles, refracts according to Snell's Law (n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂), focuses through lenses, and disperses into spectral colors.

💡 Explanation

When light encounters a boundary between two optical media, part of the wave reflects back and part enters the medium with changed wave velocity and wavelength.

  • Law of Reflection: Angle of incidence θ₁ = Angle of reflection θ₂ relative to normal.
  • Refraction: Bends toward the normal when entering a denser medium (air to glass).
  • Optical Lens: Convex lenses converge parallel rays to a common focal point F.
  • Dispersion: Prisms split white light because shorter blue wavelengths refract more than longer red wavelengths.

🔊 Sound vibrations, longitudinal compression & waveforms

Observe vibrating speaker cones or tuning forks creating longitudinal air compression and rarefaction waves, mapped directly to oscilloscope waveforms.

📖 Definition

Sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave produced by vibrating matter. It requires an elastic material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) and propagates through alternating compressions (high pressure) and rarefactions (low pressure).

💡 Explanation

Individual medium particles do not travel across the room; they oscillate back and forth about their equilibrium positions while energy propagates forward.

  • Frequency (Hz): Number of wave cycles per second; determines musical pitch.
  • Amplitude (A): Maximum particle displacement from equilibrium; determines loudness and sound pressure level.
  • Compression & Rarefaction: High density air bands vs low density air bands.

🎚️ Wave Properties

Pitch & Frequency

Higher frequency vibrations generate higher pitch tones.

Loudness & Amplitude

Greater physical vibration excursion creates higher sound pressure.

Medium Requirement

Speed of sound is ~343 m/s in air, ~1480 m/s in water, ~5120 m/s in iron.

🔄 Pressure & Gas Laws force per unit area & kinetic molecular collisions

A transparent pressure vessel with movable piston, colliding gas molecules with real-time velocity vectors, analog needle manometer, and mercury thermometer.

📖 Definition

Pressure (P) is the perpendicular force exerted per unit surface area (P = F / A), measured in Pascals (1 Pa = 1 N/m² = 1 J/m³). In gases, pressure arises from the sum of kinetic molecular collisions against container walls.

💡 Explanation

According to Kinetic Molecular Theory and the Ideal Gas Law (PV = nRT), reducing container volume or increasing thermal temperature causes molecules to collide more frequently and vigorously with container walls, driving pressure upward.

  • Boyle's Law: Pressure is inversely proportional to volume at constant temperature (P ∝ 1/V).
  • Gay-Lussac's Law: Pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature (P ∝ T).
  • Force vs Area: A smaller contact area concentrates mechanical force into extreme localized pressure.
