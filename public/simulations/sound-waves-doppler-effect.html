Sound Waves & Doppler Shift Dynamics Acoustics • Wave Mechanics • Sonic Phenomena

Sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave produced by vibrating objects that propagates through a medium (gas, liquid, or solid) via alternating compressions and rarefactions. When a sound source moves relative to an observer, the observed wave frequency changes—this is the Doppler Effect.

How Sound Waves Propagate

  • Compression: Regions of high particle density and elevated pressure where air molecules are pushed together.
  • Rarefaction: Regions of low particle density where air molecules spread apart.
  • Wavelength ($\lambda$): Distance between successive compressions ($\lambda = v / f$).
  • Amplitude: Maximum displacement of air particles; corresponds directly to loudness and decibel intensity ($dB$).

The Doppler Shift Formula

As an emitting vehicle approaches, wave crests are compressed together, yielding a higher observed pitch ($f' > f$). As it recedes, wave crests spread out, lowering the perceived pitch ($f' < f$).

$f' = f \left( \frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s} \right)$ $v = 343\text{ m/s}$ (Speed of Sound) • $v_s$ (Source velocity) • $v_o$ (Observer velocity)

Acoustic Velocity in Media

  • Air (20°C): ~343 m/s (~1235 km/h). Sound cannot travel through a vacuum because there are no particles to collide.
  • Water: ~1480 m/s (~4.3x faster than air due to greater bulk modulus).
  • Steel: ~5960 m/s (~17x faster than air due to high elastic rigidity).

Everyday Applications

  • Radar & Police Speed Guns: Doppler radar bounces microwave radio waves off moving automobiles to calculate speed.
  • Echocardiography: Medical Doppler ultrasound visualizes arterial blood flow velocity in real-time.
  • Astrophysics (Redshift): Light waves from distant galaxies exhibit Doppler red-shifting, proving cosmic expansion.

Longitudinal vs. Transverse Wave Geometry

Waves transmit energy through disturbances without bulk transport of the medium matter.

Longitudinal Waves

Particles vibrate parallel to the direction of wave propagation (e.g. sound waves, seismic P-waves).

Transverse Waves

Particles oscillate perpendicular to wave travel direction (e.g. light waves, ocean ripples, guitar strings).

Standing Waves

Interference between identical waves traveling in opposite directions creates stationary nodes and antinodes.

Human Auditory Spectrum

The human auditory system perceives frequencies between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz (20 kHz).

Infrasound (< 20 Hz)

Below human hearing threshold; produced by earthquakes, volcanoes, and used by elephants for long-distance seismic communication.

Audible Range (20 Hz - 20 kHz)

Concert pitch A4 is standardized at 440 Hz. Speech typically spans 250 Hz to 4000 Hz.

Ultrasound (> 20 kHz)

Above human perception; utilized by bats and dolphins for echolocation navigation, and in medical imaging.
