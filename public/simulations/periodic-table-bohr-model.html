Periodic Table & Bohr Atomic Shells Chemistry • General Science • Quantum Foundation

The periodic table is a tabular arrangement of chemical elements organized by atomic number (number of protons), electron configurations, and recurring chemical properties. Elements in the same group possess the same number of valence electrons and exhibit similar reactivity.

Core Principles of the Periodic Table

  • Atomic Number (Z): Uniquely identifies each element; equals total protons in the nucleus. In neutral atoms, Protons = Electrons.
  • Mass Number (A): Total sum of protons and neutrons in the nucleus ($A = Z + N$).
  • Periods (Horizontal Rows): Indicate the highest occupied energy level or quantum electron shell (K, L, M, N, O, P, Q).
  • Groups (Vertical Columns): Elements in the same group share identical valence shell configurations and chemical behaviors.

Alkali Metals (Group 1)

1 valence electron, soft, extremely reactive with water to produce hydrogen gas.

Halogens (Group 17)

7 valence electrons, highly electronegative non-metals that form salts with alkali metals.

Noble Gases (Group 18)

Full valence shells (octet 8 or duet 2), extremely stable and non-reactive inert gases.

Niels Bohr Atomic Model (1913)

In the Bohr model, electrons revolve around a positively charged central nucleus in discrete circular orbits with quantized angular momentum.

Max Electrons Per Shell: $2n^2$ Shell 1 (K): 2 • Shell 2 (L): 8 • Shell 3 (M): 18 • Shell 4 (N): 32
  • Electrons cannot spiral into the nucleus; they exist only in allowed stationary energy states.
  • When an electron absorbs energy, it jumps to a higher shell (excited state).
  • When it drops to a lower shell, it emits a photon with energy $\Delta E = h\nu$, generating spectral emission lines.

The Octet Rule & Chemical Bonding

Atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons until their outermost valence shell contains eight electrons, mimicking the electronic configuration of a stable noble gas.

  • Metals: Lose valence electrons to form positive cations ($Na \rightarrow Na^+ + e^-$).
  • Non-Metals: Gain electrons to form negative anions ($Cl + e^- \rightarrow Cl^-$) or share electrons in covalent bonds.

Real-World Applications

  • Semiconductor Physics: Silicon (Si) and Germanium (Ge) in Group 14 form the foundation of all microprocessors and solar cells.
  • Nuclear Energy: Uranium-235 and Plutonium-239 act as fissile fuels in nuclear power reactors.
  • Medicine & Diagnostics: Technetium-99m and Iodine-131 serve as radioactive medical tracers in cancer diagnostics and thyroid treatment.

Subatomic Particles & Nuclear Architecture

Every atom consists of a dense positively charged nucleus composed of protons and neutrons, enveloped by a cloud of negative electrons organized into quantum shells.

Protons ($p^+$)

Mass: $1.673 \times 10^{-27}$ kg (~1 amu). Positive charge $+1e$. Determines elemental identity.

Neutrons ($n^0$)

Mass: $1.675 \times 10^{-27}$ kg (~1 amu). Neutral charge. Provides strong nuclear force to bind protons together.

Electrons ($e^-$)

Mass: $9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg (~1/1836 amu). Negative charge $-1e$. Governs chemical reactions and bonding.

Periodic Trends Across the Table

Predictable patterns emerge across periods and groups due to effective nuclear charge ($Z_{eff}$) and atomic shielding.

Atomic Radius

Decreases left to right across a period (stronger nuclear pull); increases down a group (additional electron shells).

Ionization Energy

Increases left to right (harder to strip electrons); decreases down a group (outer electrons farther from nucleus).

Electronegativity

Fluorine (3.98) has the highest electronegativity. Increases toward top right of the table (excluding noble gases).
