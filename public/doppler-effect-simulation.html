Doppler Effect: Siren Frequency Shift for Moving Observers

What is the Doppler Effect?

The Doppler effect is the change in the pitch (frequency) of a sound that happens when the source of the sound and the listener are moving toward or away from each other.

Moving closer → higher pitch. Moving apart → lower pitch.

It is named after the Austrian scientist Christian Doppler, who described it in 1842.

Key words

Frequency: how many sound waves reach your ear each second, measured in hertz (Hz).
Pitch: how high or low a sound seems. More waves per second sounds higher.
Source: the object making the sound, like a siren.
Observer: the person listening.

Why does the pitch change?

A siren sends out sound waves in all directions. When an ambulance drives toward you, each new wave starts a little closer to you than the one before, so the waves are squeezed together. More waves reach your ear every second, so you hear a higher pitch.

When the ambulance drives away, each new wave starts farther away. The waves are stretched out, fewer reach your ear each second, and you hear a lower pitch.

The siren itself never changes. Only what you hear changes.

The formula

f′ = f × (c − vlistener) ÷ (c − vsource)

f = the siren’s real frequency, f′ = the frequency you hear, c = speed of sound (about 343 m/s in air). Each speed is measured along the line between the siren and the listener. Toward each other makes f′ bigger.
