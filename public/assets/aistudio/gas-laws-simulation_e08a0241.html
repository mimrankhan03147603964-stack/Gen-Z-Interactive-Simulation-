Boyle's Law and Charles's Law: How Gases Behave

Boyle's Law

For a fixed amount of gas at a constant temperature, when the volume goes down, the pressure goes up. They move in opposite directions.

P ∝ 1/V   or   P1V1 = P2V2

P = pressure, V = volume. Squeeze the gas into half the space and the pressure doubles.

Charles's Law

For a fixed amount of gas at a constant pressure, when the temperature goes up, the volume goes up. Hot gas takes more space.

V ∝ T   or   V1/T1 = V2/T2

T must be in Kelvin: K = °C + 273.15.

Why does Boyle's Law happen?

Gas is made of tiny particles zooming around and bumping into walls. Those bumps are the pressure.

  • Smaller space: particles hit the walls more often, so pressure rises.
  • Bigger space: hits are rarer, so pressure drops.

Everyday examples: a syringe, a bicycle pump, a scuba tank, the "pop" of a squeezed bottle.

Example: 50 mL of gas at 100 kPa is squeezed to 25 mL. P2 = 100 × 50 ÷ 25 = 200 kPa.

Why does Charles's Law happen?

Heat makes particles move faster. Faster particles push harder, so a flexible container such as a balloon stretches until the pressure is balanced again.

  • Heat the gas: balloon grows.
  • Cool the gas: balloon shrinks.

Everyday examples: a hot-air balloon, a basketball that gets soft in cold weather, bread rising in an oven.

Example: 300 mL of gas at 0°C (273 K) is heated to 100°C (373 K). V2 = 300 × 373 ÷ 273 ≈ 410 mL.
